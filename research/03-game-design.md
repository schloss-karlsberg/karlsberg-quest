# Karlsberg Quest: Design Research on "Darker, but Fun"

Research for the Game Design Document. Scope: tone (dark but enjoyable), mystery and deduction mechanics, serious and educational history games, scope and browser constraints, and age ratings (USK and PEGI).

Method: web search, then reading the pages. Only pages that actually opened are cited (see Sources). Several pages could not be read (PC Gamer, The Inlander, Screenhub, Slant and GameSkinny returned errors or truncated text; several PDFs could not be parsed), so claims that rest only on those pages have been left out. Paraphrases are mine. Short quotes are marked. Where a point is my own design inference and not a sourced claim, it says **(inference)**.

Genre borrowing only. Nothing below proposes reusing names, lore, UI or assets from the games discussed.

## 1. How "dark but fun" games balance grimness and enjoyment

### 1.1 Case notes

**Darkest Dungeon (Red Hook, 2016)**
- *Darkness comes from the systems, not from decoration.* Stress and afflictions are modelled loosely on real stress psychology: Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome and the diathesis-stress model. Stress spreads between party members. Tyler Sigman: "We all have good parts and bad parts, and we all react to stress differently." Chris Bourassa, on heroes acting on their own when afflicted: "It's frustrating to you as a player, but that's the point." (Killscreen)
- *Hope inside the system.* High stress can also produce positive "Virtues", and quirks can help as well as hurt (Wikipedia; Killscreen). Collapse is likely but not certain, which keeps the player gambling instead of despairing.
- *Gallows-humour voice.* A sardonic narrator (Wayne June) comments throughout. The developers added him after hearing his Lovecraft readings (Wikipedia). The voice turns repeated failure into theatre.
- *A safe hub.* The Hamlet is where stress is treated, afflictions are cured and heroes are recruited (Wikipedia). It is the respite between descents.
- *Where it got tiring.* Early-access players pushed back on mechanics they found too punishing (corpses, heart attacks), and Red Hook made them optional (Wikipedia). Red Hook later added "Radiant" mode, which cut a campaign from about 80 to about 40 hours by reducing the systems that "soak up the most time". It was framed as a length fix, not an easy mode (Gamewatcher). Lesson: grimness lands, but grind and length wear players out.
- Designers on intent: "We want you to feel some of these things" (Sigman). Bourassa: the game "is at its best when you feel like you're playing with a high level of risk and reward" (GamesBeat).

**Hades (Supergiant, 2020)**
- *Failure moves the story forward.* After each death you return to the house and talk to characters, and side plots advance. Kasavin wanted the story's progress after death to be "almost something to look forward to" (TheGamer). A dark mythological family tragedy becomes a warm loop.
- *Lots of "dumb stuff."* Kasavin argues that small, unnecessary, charming elements make a game special, because you can't predict which one a player will connect with (GDC podcast page).
- *Difficulty shouldn't wall off the story.* Kasavin finds it frustrating when engaged players can't see the ending because of difficulty, so Supergiant builds systems to soften that (GDC podcast page).

**Cult of the Lamb (Massive Monster, 2022)**
- *Cute style as a buffer.* Cute animals let the team "explore some extremely dark territory without being too upsetting". James Pearmain: "because it's wrapped up in this carefree aesthetic, you never feel too bad" (Inverse).
- *Contrast in gameplay as well as art.* "We wanted to have that contrast in the gameplay as well." Fast dungeon runs alternate with calm downtime, and fishing helps "wash those horrors of the dungeon crawling away" (Nintendo Life).
- *Invented iconography.* They feared real crucifixes and pentagrams would upset players, so they designed their own occult symbols: "We wanted to make our own world and our own mythology" (Inverse). This matters for us: invented symbols sidestep real-world sensitivities.
- *Daylight horror.* Pearmain cites *Midsommar*-style sunny pastoral horror (Inverse).

**Inscryption (Daniel Mullins, 2021)**
- Began as a 48-hour Ludum Dare jam game. Mullins says much of the pull sits outside the card rules: "the mysterious antagonist, the strange fixtures around the cabin, the talking cards". Escape-room puzzles in the cabin carry progress between runs, and each run feels like "another step toward a larger goal" (Game Developer).
- Takeaway: a tiny core mechanic wrapped in a strong, creepy *place* and a *presence* (someone watching you) gives a lot of tone for little content.

**Don't Starve (Klei, 2013)**
- Kevin Forbes: "we were going for something that is dark and creepy while still maintaining a strong sense of appeal." The team's reference folder was full of Burton and Gorey images, and the art is a "sketchy newspaper cartoon style" (Game Developer).
- You are dropped in with almost no instructions. Learning comes from systems exploration (Game Developer).

**Hollow Knight (Team Cherry, 2017)**
- Brief to the composer: "evoke a sense of dark elegance and melancholy." Christopher Larkin uses subtle room tone and echo. In one hub, buried crowd and traffic sounds give "a sense of memories, or what was once there before". In another track he wants the player to feel "there was once a prosperous kingdom here… but it's no longer" (Bandcamp Daily).
- Directly relevant to time travel: sound can carry the *ghost of a past era* while you stand in the present.
- *Discovery as the reward.* "We wanted players to feel like an explorer entering uncharted lands." Maps are bought from a cartographer and filled in by exploring (Game Informer). Pellen: "Just make something that you'd really want to explore" (MCV).

**Disco Elysium (ZA/UM, 2019)**
- *Fail forward.* Failed checks generate content instead of dead ends. Coverage summarised on GameBanshee says most scenarios let you "fail forward as a way of shaping your character", that mistakes are "not dead-ends", and that failure "makes victories feel much more meaningful" (GameBanshee, quoting PC Gamer).
- Takeaway: bleak setting plus failure that is funny and revealing.

**Pathologic 2 (Ice-Pick Lodge, 2019)**
- The opposite pole. The studio said that as "the game about a catastrophe, it was always intended to be grueling, stressful, and bleak". After launch they added sliders for hunger, exhaustion, infection growth and more (Game Informer).
- Takeaway: deliberate unbearability is a valid art choice but narrows the audience, and even its makers added a pressure-release valve. It is not our target.

**Sunless Sea (Failbetter, 2015)**
- Slow travel plus music builds "an atmosphere of tension". Strong literary vignettes, with writing as the main praise. Permadeath with a legacy: a successor inherits some possessions, and a will can pass on wealth. Failed skill checks can hurt and lock retries for a while (Wikipedia).
- Takeaway: a legacy system softens permadeath. For a time-travel game, what one era leaves to another is a natural fit.

**Kentucky Route Zero (Cardboard Computer)**
- The developers say their "primary concern is storytelling". They use "always-moving-forward conversations" instead of hub-and-spoke dialogue, and cite *Photopia*, which "blends fantasy and the mundane in a really touching way" (Funambulism interview).
- Takeaway: melancholy magical realism, where the uncanny is treated as ordinary, is a cheap and powerful tone lever.

**Spiritfarer (Thunder Lotus, 2020)**
- A management game about ferrying the dead. Critics describe it as tackling "the heavy topic of death" while staying "colorful" and "feel-good". The creative director drew on personal loss, and the team researched end-of-life care. Some critics found its tasks repetitive (Wikipedia).
- Takeaway: melancholy works when it is personal, researched and paired with caring verbs (cook, build, hug). Repetition is still the risk.

**Return of the Obra Dinn, Pentiment and Heaven's Vault** are covered in sections 2 and 3.

### 1.2 Cozy dread and folk horror as frameworks

- **Cozy horror and cozy dread.** The Strong Museum of Play describes it as scares without stakes. Typical traits are cozy core loops (gathering, shopkeeping), friendly settings, atmospheric staging with cool colours and ambient music, odd NPC warnings, and "subtle rather than graphic horror", which is suggestion and flavour text instead of jump scares. Examples: Dredge, Strange Horticulture. Their critic notes that "real scares live in the shadows, at the edge of view", and that repeated encounters blunt dread (Museum of Play).
- Superjump lists the traits: soft storybook visuals, puzzles that don't need fast reflexes, threats that feel personal and manageable, **familiar places that look different on a second visit**, mysteries left partly unexplained, and an ebb and flow between exploration and scares (Superjump). The second-visit point is an exact match for time travel: the same hilltop in 1793, 1890 and today.
- **Folk horror.** Adam Scovell's "folk horror chain" is landscape, isolation, skewed belief systems and morality, and "happening / summoning". James Thurgill uses Relph's *topophobia* and argues that folk horror treats landscape as an *active participant*: rural places are "simultaneously innocuous yet malign" (Revenant Journal). The Saarpfalz woods, sandstone, burial mounds and the burned palace site fit this naturally. The horror can come from the land and its layered past, not from monsters.

### 1.3 What makes a dark tone compelling and what makes it tiring

Compelling, from the evidence above:
1. **Agency inside the darkness.** The player acts against or through the grimness: Darkest Dungeon's risk gambling, resisting in Through the Darkest of Times, solving in Obra Dinn.
2. **Hope or warmth somewhere.** Virtues in Darkest Dungeon, family repair in Hades, a safe hub. Graham McNeill (Trench Crusade) wants "the little tiny bit of hope left at the end" and rejects "grimdark misery porn". Gore and terror must be "*for* something" (Wargamer).
3. **A wry voice.** Darkest Dungeon's narrator, Cult of the Lamb's jokes, Disco Elysium's funny failures.
4. **Contrast and rhythm.** Dungeon then fishing, descent then hamlet, dread then cozy.
5. **Mystery.** Darkness as the unknown you want to understand (Hollow Knight, Obra Dinn, folk horror) is pleasurable. Darkness as unavoidable punishment is not.
6. **Suggestion over depiction.** Cheaper to make, lower age rating, and often scarier.

Tiring:
- **Grind and length.** Darkest Dungeon's Radiant mode existed to fix this, and Spiritfarer's critics flagged repetitive tasks.
- **Unrelenting punishment** with no release valve. Pathologic 2 added sliders after launch, and Darkest Dungeon made its harshest mechanics optional.
- **Bleakness without meaning or variation** (McNeill).
- **Repetition kills dread**: monsters seen too often stop being scary (Museum of Play on Dredge).
- **Difficulty walls in front of story** (Kasavin).

## 2. Mystery and deduction mechanics for a history and archaeology game

### 2.1 Return of the Obra Dinn: structured deduction
- Loop: find remains, use a device to see the frozen moment of death (with preceding audio), then record identity, cause of death and killer in a logbook. The logbook also holds drawings, a crew roster and a ship plan (Wikipedia; Emily Short).
- **Anti-guessing check:** correct fates are confirmed in **sets of three** (two near the end) "to deter guesswork" (Wikipedia). Pope also allowed loose matching for cause of death to avoid frustration over minor details (Automaton interview).
- Emily Short on why it works: "the work you're doing in the game is also the work required to understand the story." The fun "was very much associated with learning the system of life on the ship." Access to areas is gated by discoveries, not keys. Her criticisms: the last identifications become "laborious and fiddly", some deaths require overly precise cause-of-death guesses, and a commenter notes that players guess identities from national stereotypes (Emily Short blog).
- **Solo-dev reality:** about 4.5 years. Pope says he "made it too hard". The journal was added **mid-development** once he realised how hard a non-chronological story with 60+ characters is to follow. Instead of much playtesting he relied on a self-built tool that analysed the clue graph (unconnected clue nodes, when each character becomes identifiable). His design goal was that players can see most of the game without solving anything, and curiosity pulls them into solving (Automaton interview). He planned an in-fiction hint system and dropped it because "I couldn't get a good metaphor going" (Automaton news).

**Fit for Karlsberg Quest:** a "Fates of the Site" ledger per location (who was here, what happened, when). Clues come from LiDAR relief, finds, archival snippets and short past-era scenes. Confirm in batches. The clue-graph tooling and the notebook have to be **day-one features**, not late additions.

### 2.2 Heaven's Vault: interpretation and translation
- Each inscription is a guess. The game advises but doesn't confirm. Ingold: "we won't tell you if you're right or wrong"; "We're not at school here! We're not filling in an exam!"; with no confirmation, supporting evidence feels earned: "It's yours, you get to own that" (The Sixth Axis).
- Why: "If we'd confirmed the player's translations, then players would have brute-forced them." This mirrors archaeology: "You can't ever know if you've interpreted a find, or a culture, correctly." "The whole language is the puzzle: each individual inscription is just a part of that puzzle." They set it in a fictional world because players would already know the history of a real one such as Egypt (Game Developer).
- Ingold expects players to finish confident in only about a third of the language (The Sixth Axis). The game won the IGF Excellence in Narrative award, with mixed-to-good scores of Metacritic 76 on PC and 71 on PS4 (Wikipedia).
- **Tension for us:** a real-history game must teach *correct* facts, while Heaven's Vault deliberately leaves things uncertain. The fix **(inference)** is to split two layers: (a) *settled* facts (dates, stratigraphy, what a LiDAR feature is), confirmed Obra Dinn-style, and (b) *interpretations* (why was this mound built here? what did the duke's subjects feel?), which stay open and are discussed with characters. This is honest archaeology and good play.

### 2.3 Pentiment: investigation without a canonical answer
- Sawyer: "no canonical true killer, so we won't be telling the player they got it wrong", only "here are the consequences". "There's not like a high burden of proof in this era." An alibi-logic prototype "wasn't what we wanted". Accusations follow you: "People will remember that you're the person who pinned the crime on a certain person" (TheGamer).
- Time pressure limits how many leads you can follow, so you can't see everything in one playthrough (Wikipedia). Dialogue options depend on your background ("If you lived in Italy, you know Italian"), and showing off can make you "come across as a know-it-all" (TheGamer). This makes knowledge part of who you are.
- Three historians consulted (de Hamel, Kern, Black). Sawyer writes to them "and ask[s] them if something is plausible" (TheGamer). The team started as two people and grew to 13. It won a Peabody and the GDCA Best Narrative award. Some critics found it slow (Wikipedia).

### 2.4 Outer Wilds: knowledge as the only key
- Beachum: "the sole purpose of exploration is to answer questions about the world you're in." The time loop "was invented to make repeated playthroughs diegetic", and a 20-minute simulation window makes ambitious dynamic systems possible. The world "constantly makes references to things players can actually go out and discover". On tension: "the decision to investigate the cave is a lot more interesting if you're worried about what you might find inside" (Game Developer).
- Curiosity design: "the feeling of seeing something in the distance and you choose to go there and being rewarded"; "We're trying to avoid explicit signs as much as possible"; "we give you enough information that you make conscious choices" (Mobius Digital).
- **Fit:** time travel between eras is a diegetic loop. Progress is gated by understanding: "I now know this LiDAR ridge is a medieval field boundary, so I know where the lost path ran in 1350." No keys, no level-ups.

### 2.5 Which mechanics make learning real facts feel like play
Synthesis from the sources:
1. **The fact is the key.** Knowledge unlocks places or outcomes (Outer Wilds, Obra Dinn's discovery gating). A fact you needed sticks better than one you collected.
2. **The player commits to a hypothesis** before getting confirmation (Obra Dinn's ledger, Heaven's Vault's guesses, Pentiment's accusations). Commitment creates stakes.
3. **Confirm in batches or give partial feedback** so guessing doesn't pay (Obra Dinn's threes).
4. **The notebook is the game's main interface.** The player's understanding is written down and inspectable (Obra Dinn's logbook, Heaven's Vault's lexicon).
5. **Knowledge changes conversations** (Pentiment's background-gated options, Heaven's Vault translations fed into dialogue).
6. **Avoid detached trivia.** Valiant Hearts' and Discovery Tour's collectible fact cards are the weak form (see section 3). Facts not built into play work less well.

## 3. Serious and educational history and archaeology games: what worked and what failed

**Assassin's Creed Discovery Tour (Ubisoft)**
- Removed combat and death, then added 75 guided tours built with Egyptologists, plus hands-on activities such as making bread. Some tours flag where the game's fiction departs from history (Engadget).
- Evidence: in one class study reported by Engadget, 55% of traditional-lesson students passed the follow-up quiz against 42% of Discovery Tour students (22% at pre-test). Ubisoft's own account of the Université de Montréal study (400 students) calls outcomes "practically identical, even though the DT students didn't have a teacher", and says the "ideal scenario is for teachers to use the DT in a classroom setting" (Ubisoft Montréal). The two framings differ. Both agree the tour teaches, and that it doesn't beat a teacher.
- For the Greece edition they added guides who quiz you at the end, rewards and avatars, informed by teacher feedback (Ubisoft Montréal).
- Takeaway: a strong world plus passive tours gives modest learning. Interaction and a mediating guide matter.

**Attentat 1942 (Charles Games / Charles University, about 2 hours)**
- You interview fictional witnesses about your grandfather's deportation after the Heydrich assassination. Witnesses reveal more when you confront them with new information, and diaries steer conversations. Graphic-novel cutscenes are paced by the player (bpb).
- The bpb reviewer's critique: thin gameplay, mostly watching and listening. Polish *suggests* accuracy while the quest log mixes real and invented people, so teachers need to address fact versus fiction. Used alone it raises questions students can't resolve. The reviewer suggests 12+ (bpb).
- Takeaway: confronting witnesses with evidence is a great investigation mechanic, but **label fiction versus fact clearly** in-game.

**Through the Darkest of Times (Paintbucket, 2020)**
- You lead a resistance group in Berlin from 1933 to 1945. Each turn opens with historically accurate newspaper clippings, and scenes show everyday citizens. Player choices don't change major history but do affect later events. The art draws on 1920s Expressionism (Wikipedia).
- USK: the gamescom demo received **USK 12** in August 2018, the first rating for a game with swastikas after the USK began applying the social-adequacy clause to games. The reasoning was that all player actions are directed **against** the Nazi regime and that 12-year-olds can recognise the ethical message (GamePro). (Wikipedia names the Wolfenstein titles of 2019 as the first *released* games under the clause, so "first" depends on rated versus released.)
- Reception: strong writing and art, but strategy too simple, repetitive, and choices weakly tied to the narrative (Wikipedia).
- Takeaway: **perspective** (ordinary people, resistance) makes very dark material acceptable and teachable. The game loop still has to carry its weight.

**This War of Mine (11 bit studios)**
- Put on Poland's optional school reading list in 2020. It was chosen for its civilian perspective on war, not soldiers', and the Prime Minister cited its reflection and "space for an interesting analysis of events" (Notes from Poland).
- Takeaway: dark material is legitimised by perspective and reflection, not by spectacle.

**Valiant Hearts (Ubisoft Montpellier)** — the educator review page didn't open, so this game is not used as evidence here.

**Pentiment** is in section 2.3: consulting historians, everyday medieval life ("medieval peasants are just like us", per the Vice podcast summary), and dark events (executions, rebellion, a burning abbey) shown through the lives of a community over decades (Wikipedia; Vice).

**Heaven's Vault** is in section 2.2. It shows archaeology as interpretation instead of Indiana Jones treasure hunting.

**Museum and heritage location-based games**
- Jones, Theodosis and Lykourentzou (2019) studied a location-based city-history game. "Enthusiasts" and "Interested" players valued free route choice and autonomy. "Sceptics" and "Cynics" were intolerant of technical problems and expected polish (Utrecht University research portal).
- Takeaway for a browser game: polish and stability decide whether sceptical adults, such as teachers and parents, accept the experience.

### 3.1 How serious games handle dark history respectfully (synthesis)
1. **Perspective of the ordinary and the affected**, not the perpetrator: TTDOT (resistance), This War of Mine (civilians), Attentat (witnesses and families).
2. **Player actions don't glorify violence.** The USK's reasoning for TTDOT rested on this (GamePro).
3. **Grounded in sources, with experts consulted** (Pentiment's three historians, Discovery Tour's Egyptologists, Attentat's historians and testimonies).
4. **Fiction marked as fiction** (Discovery Tour flags myths; the main critique of Attentat was the blurred line).
5. **Reflection over spectacle**: newspapers, diaries, letters, consequences that follow you.
6. **Invented symbols** where real ones would be gratuitous (Cult of the Lamb's approach, carried over **(inference)**).

## 4. Dark tone in browser games and scope for small teams

### 4.1 Browser constraints that shape tone
- **Audio needs a user gesture.** Chrome blocks autoplay with sound, and since Chrome 71 this covers Web Audio. An `AudioContext` created before a gesture starts "suspended" and must be `resume()`d on a click or tap (Chrome for Developers). **(inference)** Turn the required click into a diegetic ritual such as "light the lantern" or "put on the headphones" that starts the soundscape. Sound carries much of the dark atmosphere (Hollow Knight's room tone), so this moment matters.
- **First seconds and file size.** Web-portal best practice (Defold's Poki guidance): total size "under 20MB", "Gameplay within seconds", "Can the player start having fun within ten seconds?", avoid "long intros, dialogue-heavy tutorials, complex menus", and aim for 5+ minutes average playtime, with 7–10+ very strong (Defold). Karlsberg Quest won't be a portal game, but the same impatience applies to any link someone clicks.
- **Darkness versus readability (inference).** Real darkness on phone screens and in bright rooms makes things unreadable. Get dread from fog, silhouettes, colour temperature, sound and *what is implied*, not from crushed blacks. Fog and limited draw distance are also performance wins in three.js.
- **Sessions.** Design 5–15 minute "case beats" that save automatically (inference from the portal guidance and the Obra Dinn and Outer Wilds loop lengths).

### 4.2 Scope lessons
- **Hollow Knight**, a core team of three: ambition outgrew the plan, and whole areas were cut ("Cutting it was tough at the time"). "'Keep it simple' was our visual goal" made a large world feasible (Game Informer; MCV).
- **Obra Dinn**, solo: about 4.5 years, exhaustion, "made it too hard", journal bolted on mid-development, little playtesting (Automaton). Teaches: prototype the notebook and difficulty first, and playtest early with non-designers. Pope only saw the real difficulty when his wife played.
- **Inscryption**: a 48-hour jam core grew into a multi-year project (Game Developer). A tiny, proven core loop first, atmosphere around it second.
- **Pentiment**: started as two people for a long stretch before growing (Wikipedia). A narrow, strong vertical concept can be built small.
- **Darkest Dungeon**: had to reduce campaign length after launch (Gamewatcher). Plan for *short* from the start.
- Generic scoping advice found during research was mostly low-authority blog content, so none of it is relied on here. The consistent message of the primary cases above: **cut, don't extend; one complete slice before breadth.**

**Recommended vertical slice (inference):** one real location (for example the Karlsberg plateau), **two** eras (1793 night of the fire and today, with LiDAR), **one** "fates" case of 5–8 entries, one wry guide voice, one ambient soundscape per era, about 20–30 minutes of play. Ship and test that before adding Celtic, medieval, 19th-century or 20th-century content.

## 5. Audience and rating: how dark is OK for whom

### 5.1 PEGI (Europe-wide; definitions from pegi.info)
- **PEGI 7:** may "possibly be frightening to younger children"; only very mild, implied or non-realistic violence. The "Fear" descriptor applies at 7.
- **PEGI 12:** "slightly more graphic" violence towards fantasy characters, non-realistic violence towards human-like characters, mild bad language. **"Horror" at 12 = "moderate horror sequences or disturbing images".**
- **PEGI 16:** violence that "looks the same as would be expected in real life", stronger language, tobacco, alcohol or drug depiction. **Horror at 16 = "intense and sustained" horror sequences** (with or without violence).
- **PEGI 18:** gross violence, motiveless killing, violence against defenceless people. Discriminatory stereotypes likely to encourage hatred are always 18.
- Interactive-risk notes: daily or streak rewards push towards PEGI 7, and losing rewards for not returning in time towards PEGI 12 (pegi.info). **Avoid FOMO mechanics.**

### 5.2 USK (Germany)
- Categories 0 / 6 / 12 / 16 / 18. The ratings say when a game is safe from a youth-protection view, **not** whether it is pedagogically suitable (usk.de).
- Content descriptors since 2023 that are directly relevant to a "darker" history game (usk.de):
  - **Düstere Atmosphäre** (dark atmosphere): "The game environment is dark, oppressive and/or can appear threatening and frightening."
  - **Schreckmomente** (scary moments): scary elements or sudden frightening effects that may disturb children.
  - **Belastende Themen** (burdensome themes): emotionally stressful topics such as death.
  - **Horror**: relevant horror or disgust effects.
  - Violence tiers: **Angedeutete Gewalt** (implied, abstract), **Fantasy-Gewalt**, **Gewalt** (realistic or prominent), **Drastische Gewalt** (clearly visualised or overly focused), and **Kriegsthematik** (war setting).
- Plain-language summaries: USK 12 games "can already be significantly more combat-oriented or darker in design"; USK 16 often has realistic violence and story-framed armed combat (Elternguide). Wikipedia's summary: 12 has "little violence, so players can distance themselves"; 18 is often a "dark atmosphere" combined with brutal violence (Wikipedia, USK).
- **Nazi-era imagery** (relevant to the 20th-century era): until 2018 the USK refused games with unconstitutional symbols (§86a StGB). Since August 2018 it decides case by case under the social-adequacy clause. TTDOT got USK 12 because all player actions oppose the regime (GamePro; Wikipedia).
- **Online and browser distribution:** since a 2021 reform, labelling duties extend to online gaming platforms. On 25 April 2025 the German states officially recognised IARC ratings, which the USK issues for app and online stores (heise). Self-hosted browser games fall under the Interstate Treaty on the Protection of Minors in the Media (JMStV), not the boxed-product regime. The KJM 2026 infosheet that sets out provider duties could not be read here (PDF), so treat the exact obligations as **to be verified** and not as legal advice.

### 5.3 What fits whom (inference from the descriptors above)
| Target | Allowed darkness | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Families / USK 6 / PEGI 7** | Mild eeriness, melancholy, ruins, "something was lost here" | Scary moments, death shown on screen, oppressive atmosphere |
| **Teens and adults / USK 12 / PEGI 12** — *recommended* | Dark atmosphere, occasional (not sustained) scares, death as a theme, implied or historical violence, war as backdrop, executions and fires reported or shown off-screen or stylised, moderate disturbing images | Realistic violence on screen, sustained horror, gore, playable atrocity, real extremist symbols as decoration |
| **USK 16 / PEGI 16** | Sustained horror, realistic violence | Doesn't serve the learning goal, excludes school use and narrows the audience |

The sweet spot for "a bit darker but fun" and still classroom-usable is **USK 12 / PEGI 12**, with expected descriptors around *Düstere Atmosphäre*, *Schreckmomente*, *Belastende Themen*, and at most *Angedeutete Gewalt*. A "gentle mode" with fewer scares and more hints could keep it accessible to younger players with a parent or teacher (inference, informed by Pathologic's sliders and Darkest Dungeon's Radiant mode).

## 6. Lessons we take for Karlsberg Quest

1. **Dark world, warm loop.** Keep the setting grim (a burned palace, plague and war years, graves in the forest) but make every return to the present feel like coming home: a safe hub such as the field station or archive, where new knowledge is sorted and characters react. Hades turns death into "something to look forward to", and Darkest Dungeon has its Hamlet.
2. **One wry, humane guide voice.** An original companion or narrator with gallows humour, in the spirit of Darkest Dungeon's narrator and Disco Elysium's funny failures. They comment on your mistakes and the grimness without mocking real victims. Humour is aimed at pomp (the duke's vanity), bureaucracy and the player. Never at the suffering.
3. **Deduction is the core verb.** For each site, a "fates" ledger: who was here, what happened, when, and the evidence. Confirm in batches of three so guessing doesn't pay, and accept loose wording (Obra Dinn). Build the notebook and a clue-graph checker in week one. Pope added his mid-development and called the result too hard.
4. **Separate facts from interpretations.** Settled facts (dates, LiDAR feature types, stratigraphy) get confirmed. Interpretations (why here? what did people feel?) stay open and are debated with characters, as in Heaven's Vault. This is honest archaeology and avoids "We're not filling in an exam!" Clearly mark invented characters and scenes as fiction (the Attentat 1942 critique).
5. **Knowledge is the only key.** No keycards or levels. Reading a LiDAR hillshade correctly or dating a wall reveals where to go in another era (Outer Wilds: "the sole purpose of exploration is to answer questions"). Show distant landmarks and avoid explicit signs.
6. **Time travel is our "second visit" for dread.** Cozy horror's strongest trick is familiar places turned strange. The same Karlsberg terrace calm in 2026, burning in 1793 and overgrown in 1890. Use sound for the ghost of an era, like Hollow Knight's buried crowd noise: suggestion over depiction, scares at the edge of view and rarely repeated.
7. **Folk-horror landscape, not monsters.** Let the real Saarpfalz terrain carry the unease: barrows, sandstone hollows, fog in the Bliestal, a palace that is now forest. That is Scovell and Thurgill's "innocuous yet malign" landscape. Any ritual or occult element uses **invented** symbols (Cult of the Lamb's approach) and stays clearly separate from real archaeology.
8. **Respectful darkness through perspective.** Dark history is told through ordinary people such as servants, villagers, soldiers' families and refugees, using sources (letters, records). The player never commits atrocities and never acts on the perpetrators' side (TTDOT and This War of Mine). For the 20th-century era, avoid extremist symbols entirely or treat them only as documentary context. Have a historian review sensitive scripts, as Pentiment did.
9. **Target USK 12 / PEGI 12.** Dark atmosphere, occasional scary moments, death as a theme, violence implied or off-screen. No gore, no sustained horror, no realistic violence (those push it to 16). No streak or FOMO mechanics. Add a "gentle mode" (fewer jump moments, more hints) so families and classes can use it. For distribution, verify current JMStV and IARC duties (IARC recognised April 2025).
10. **Rhythm against fatigue.** Alternate tense investigation beats with calm ones: sorting finds, sketching, a walk in the present. Keep case beats to 5–15 minutes and campaigns short. Darkest Dungeon had to halve its length after launch, and grind is the number-one tone killer.
11. **Browser-first atmosphere.** Fun within about 10 seconds and an initial download around 20 MB or less. Make the audio-unlock click a diegetic ritual such as "light the lantern". Build dread from fog, silhouettes and sound, not near-black screens that fail on phones. Auto-save every beat.
12. **Scope ruthlessly: one slice first.** Vertical slice: Karlsberg, two eras (1793 and today with LiDAR), one 5–8-entry case, one guide voice, about 25 minutes. Cut, don't extend. Playtest early with non-designers, especially teens and a teacher. Polish over breadth, because sceptical adults judge heritage games on stability and finish (Jones et al.).
