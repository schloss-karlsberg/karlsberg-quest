# Karlsberg Quest: Historical research on dark and eerie material

Research date: 2026-11-02. All sources were opened and read. Snippet-only leads are kept apart in section 9.

**Labels used in this file**
- **[FACT]**: documented history, with its source.
- **[CONTESTED]**: sources disagree. Both readings are given.
- **[LEGEND]**: folklore, a nickname, an anecdote or a tourist-board claim. It is not established history.
- **[FICTION]**: invented for the game, in the hooks section only.

## 1. Duke Karl II. August of Pfalz-Zweibrücken (1746–1795) and Schloss Karlsberg

### 1.1 Personality and reputation
- **[FACT]** Hans Christoph von Gagern described him as *"ein finsterer, wollüstiger, zur Härte geneigter eigensinniger Mann"* ("a gloomy, lustful, harsh-leaning, headstrong man"). Gagern also credited him with a sense of his rank and duties.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August ; https://www.rosalux.de/news/id/692/der-karlsberg-von-barocker-pracht-zur-baustoffhandlung
- **[FACT]** The 19th-century ADB (Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie) describes:
  - a "merkwürdige Mischung von Esprit und bizarrer Pedanterie" (an odd mix of wit and bizarre pedantry);
  - a ruler devoted to "frivolen Ausschweifungen und schimmernden Nichtigkeiten" (frivolous excesses and glittering trifles).
  - Source: https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/ADB:Karl_II._(Herzog_von_Pfalz-Zweibr%C3%BCcken)
- **[FACT]** Contemporaries, and later democrats, portrayed him as the archetypal despotic, absolutist tyrant.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August
- **[FACT]** Daily rules around him, as reported by the Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung piece:
  - Only the duke could ride up to the palace. Everyone else had to walk.
  - Singing was strictly forbidden. Offenders risked, at best, a beating with a stick.
  - A contemporary said the region seemed "wie betäubt" ("as if stunned").
  - Source: https://www.rosalux.de/news/id/692/der-karlsberg-von-barocker-pracht-zur-baustoffhandlung
- **[FACT]** The nickname **"Hundskarl"** ("Dog-Karl") was popularised in 1923 by the local historian Fritz Schunck, after the Bavarian monarchy fell.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August ; Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung (above)
- **[LEGEND / anecdote]** A local hobby history site gives two stories, neither sourced:
  - The nickname comes from an incident in which one of his dogs attacked a boy. The duke would not let it be stopped and called the servant back with *"Lass dem Tierchen sein Pläsierchen"* ("let the little beast have its fun").
  - A man named Heinrich got 25 lashes for crowding the ducal coach with his own cart.
  - Source: http://nhp-2000.de/homburg-im-bild/html/body_-_vita_karl_ii_august_christia.html
  - Treat both as local tradition, not verified fact.

### 1.2 Extravagance, debts and hunting
- **[FACT]** He started with austerity measures after succeeding in 1775. Later his court grew and he went heavily into debt. He left his brother Maximilian debts of **over 9 million Gulden**.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August
- **[FACT]** According to the ADB, building and running Karlsberg and its park cost **almost 14 million Gulden**, far beyond the duchy's income.
  - Source: ADB (above)
- **[LEGEND]** Workers and the people of Homburg allegedly called the hill **"Tränenberg"** ("hill of tears") because wages were paid late or not at all. His heirs allegedly paid off his debts until about 1850.
  - Source: nhp-2000.de hobby site (above)
- **[CONTESTED]** Was the building done by corvée (unpaid labour owed to the lord)?
  - The hobby site says the work was paid: "keine Fron- sondern Lohnarbeiten".
  - The burden on subjects is documented instead for hunting: game fences, par-force hunts, and subjects having to feed the hound packs.
  - Sources: nhp-2000.de ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August
- **[FACT]** A satirical piece in Schlözer's *Stats-Anzeigen* (1782) said one province was "ganz und gar gleichsam ein einziges JagdRevier" (essentially one single hunting ground). It added that the prince kept "ganze Regimenter JagdHunde" (whole regiments of hunting dogs).
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August ; Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung
- **[FACT]** Gagern, as cited in the ADB, wrote of "tausend Pferde im Marstall, noch mehr Hunde in den Zwingern" (a thousand horses in the stables, even more dogs in the kennels). He called the land a game park "zum Verderben der Unterthanen" (to the ruin of the subjects).
  - Source: ADB
- **[FACT]** The city of Homburg describes the complex as:
  - more than 1 km wide;
  - with a Marstall (stables) and riding arena for 1,000 horses, an Orangerie with a theatre, Kavaliershäuser, barracks, a menagerie, cascades, ponds, the "Bärenzwinger" and the Tschifflik pavilion.
  - Source: https://www.homburg.de/leben-in-homburg/touristisches/sehenswertes/schloss-karlsberg/

### 1.3 The menagerie and the "Bärenzwinger"
- **[FACT]** From 1778 the duke built up an animal collection in his private park, the **Karlslust**. It held:
  - parrots, pelicans, cassowaries, monkeys and jaguars;
  - wolves, Canadian foxes, two dromedaries and porcupines;
  - later, bears.
  - Source: https://www.saarpfalz-touristik.de/attraktionen/baerenzwinger-b8c8db2c7d
- **[FACT]** The animals lived in a ring of **19 small slate-roofed houses** around a moat with a central island. Pavilion foundations were found on the island in **2016**.
  - The court painter Mannlich compared the ring to "ein antikes Amphitheater" (an ancient amphitheatre).
  - Only invited guests could see it.
  - Source: Saarpfalz-Touristik (above)
- **[FACT]** The name "Bärenzwinger" (bear pit) is a folk name and is misleading.
  - The bears were kept in reinforced iron "Bären-Kästen" (bear boxes) on the nearby Herzogsterrassen, added in 1786 and heated from outside.
  - Wikipedia describes the ring cages as aviaries.
  - The city's hiking leaflet says it is not proven that bears were ever kept there.
  - Sources: Saarpfalz-Touristik ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg ; https://www.homburg.de/media/scllyew0/schlossbergtour.pdf

### 1.4 Building the palace
- **[CONTESTED]** Building dates differ by source:
  - Wikipedia: 1778–1788.
  - City of Homburg: 1776–1786.
  - Both agree the residence moved there in **1779**.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg ; homburg.de (above)
- **[FACT]** On 27 July 1777 the duke bought the Louisenhof estate on the Buchenberg for 35,000 Gulden plus 60 Louis d'or. The hill was renamed Karlsberg.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg
- **[FACT]** On **19 December 1785** a fireworks explosion at Karlsberg killed several people. Their names are not given.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg
- **[FACT]** The valley was previously called **"Teufelsloch"** ("devil's hole") because of its gloomy, wild landscape. The cellar of the Kavaliersgebäude is nicknamed **"Räuberhöhle"** ("robbers' cave").
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg
- **[FACT]** Emperor Joseph II reportedly said he had never seen comparable splendour. Adolph von Knigge (1792) called Karlsberg a "Feen-Schloß" ("fairy castle").
  - Sources: ADB ; https://www.literaturland-saar.de/orte/homburg/

### 1.5 The flight in 1793
- **[FACT]** On **9 February 1793** the duke was warned by **Nicolaus (Nikel) Pfeiffer**, a farmer and postal worker from Rohrbach (now part of St. Ingbert). He escaped French troops "um Haaresbreite" (by a hair's breadth) and fled via Kaiserslautern to Mannheim. His minister **Esebeck** was arrested and taken to Paris.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August
- **[CONTESTED]** The ADB dates the flight to **3 February 1793**, from "Sansculotten" (Sans-culottes) who had entered Zweibrücken.
  - Source: ADB
- **[LEGEND / unsourced detail]** He left at night by coach with torchbearers, about two hours ahead of the French. The servants left behind told the French the torches had been "Kalkfeuer" (lime-kiln fires).
  - Source: nhp-2000.de
- **[FACT]** Paris planned to put him on trial, as it had Louis XVI. The likely outcome was the guillotine.
  - Sources: Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung ; https://www.sr-mediathek.de/index.php?seite=7&id=142850

### 1.6 The destruction (28 July 1793)
- **[FACT]** After the flight, Karlsberg changed hands between Prussian and French troops several times. While the Prussians held it, the furnishings were taken away towards Mannheim.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg
- **[FACT]** After the French surrendered at Mainz, the French relief army withdrew through the Glan valley.
  - On **28 July 1793** the palace was opened to general plundering and set on fire that evening.
  - Up to **100 wagonloads of straw** were reportedly brought from near Metz.
  - By the next morning the French had gone, and local people began plundering what was left.
  - The fire mainly destroyed the duke's own residence and the timber-framed buildings.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg
- **[FACT]** One literary-history source says the destruction found "breite Unterstützung bei der heimischen Bevölkerung" (broad support among the local population).
  - Theodor Fontane quotes Prussian officer Otto von Rohr on Homburg during the 1792–93 campaign: *"Eine Hölle war uns dieser Aufenthalt"* ("this stay was a hell to us").
  - Source: https://www.literaturland-saar.de/orte/homburg/
- **[FACT]** Life on the site did not stop at once. Births are recorded at Karlsberg in November 1793, and household accounts run until 1797.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg

### 1.7 Death, and what remained
- **[FACT]** He died on **1 April 1795** after a stroke, in exile. His age is not given here: the ADB says "not yet 46", which does not match the 1746 birth year, so leave it out or check it. His sarcophagus is in the Wittelsbach crypt of St. Michael's in Munich.
  - On place of death: the ADB and Wikipedia say Mannheim.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August ; ADB
- **[CONTESTED]** The hobby site gives his place of death as Rohrbach near Heidelberg ("bei Frau Sartorie").
  - Source: nhp-2000.de
- **[FACT]** His brother Maximilian succeeded him, became Elector of Bavaria in 1799 and later the first King of Bavaria.
  - Source: nhp-2000.de ; consistent with https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August
- **[FACT]** Under Napoleon the ruins went back to the duke's widow, on condition that they be demolished to ground level. The **Baumagazin** is the only surviving building. It later served as a brewery, a forester's house and a Karlsberg Brauerei director's villa.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg
- **[FACT]** The ruin became a cheap building-materials supplier for many houses in Homburg and the area. Hence the article's title: "Von barocker Pracht zur Baustoffhandlung" ("from baroque splendour to builders' merchant").
  - Source: Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung
- **[FACT]** The furnishings were scattered to the Residenz Munich, Nymphenburg, Bamberg, Würzburg, Berchtesgaden and elsewhere. Mannlich's painting collection became one of the foundations of the **Alte Pinakothek** in Munich.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloss_Karlsberg ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_II._August
- **[FACT]** Visible relics today, along the "Schlossbergtour" trail:
  - the remains of the Orangerie;
  - the clearing of the Ehrenhof (cour d'honneur), where the main building stood;
  - the Karlsbergweiher, the Hirschbrunnen and the Schwanenweiher;
  - the "Herzogskastanien", chestnut trees over 200 years old and protected as natural monuments;
  - the ring of the Bärenzwinger.
  - Source: https://www.homburg.de/media/scllyew0/schlossbergtour.pdf
- **[FACT]** SR (Saarländischer Rundfunk) describes the site as "ein paar wenige Ruinen in einem verwunschen anmutendem Wald" (a few ruins in a forest that seems enchanted). It says "Mythen und Legenden" (myths and legends) surround the vanished palace, above all the claim that it rivalled Versailles.
  - Source: https://www.sr-mediathek.de/index.php?seite=7&id=142850
- **[FACT]** Literary reception:
  - Ernst Pasqué, *Der Karlsberg* (1875 novel);
  - Paul Gurk, *Serenissimus* (1940);
  - Ralf Kurz, *Der Diplomat*;
  - Reiner Marx's reader (2010);
  - Friedrich Aulenbach's Romantic "ruin cult". He lived in the surviving building.
  - Source: https://www.literaturland-saar.de/orte/homburg/

## 2. Homburg and the Hohenburg (Schlossberg)

- **[FACT]** The castle dates from the 12th century and is attributed to Dietrich I. The Hohenburg was the seat of the Counts of Homburg.
  - In 1330 Emperor Ludwig the Bavarian granted the town its charter.
  - The comital line died out in **1449**. A feud between Nassau-Saarbrücken and Pfalz-Zweibrücken over the castle followed until **1492**. Nassau-Saarbrücken won.
  - Sources: https://www.burgenwelt.org/deutschland/hohenburg/burgenwelt-hohenburg-objektseite.pdf ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hohenburg_Castle_(Homburg)
- **[FACT]** In the 16th century it was rebuilt as a Renaissance palace, and from 1559 converted into a fortress. Count Johann IV of Saarbrücken died on the Hohenburg in 1574.
  - Source: Burgenwelt
- **[FACT]** Thirty Years' War and after:
  - **1636**: imperial troops took the heavily fortified fortress (Burgenwelt).
  - **1641**: it was handed to Duke Charles IV of Lorraine, whose troops stayed long after the Peace of Westphalia.
  - **2 August 1671**: it passed to a Kurtrier garrison (Wikipedia).
  - Sources: Burgenwelt ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg
- **[FACT]** From **1679 to 1692**, Louis XIV's engineer **Vauban** turned the castle and town into a strong fortress. The town got its first wall, and most of the old-town layout dates from this period.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg ; Burgenwelt
- **[FACT]** The fortress was razed in **1697**, after the Peace of Rijswijk.
  - From 1701 to 1714 it was occupied first by the Dutch, then by the French, who rebuilt it.
  - It was **finally destroyed in 1714**.
  - Sources: Burgenwelt ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg
- **[FACT]** In 1755 the Homburg area passed to Pfalz-Zweibrücken. Karl II. August then used the ruin **as a quarry for Schloss Karlsberg**. The Hohenburg's stones went into the palace that was itself burned in 1793.
  - Sources: Burgenwelt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hohenburg_Castle_(Homburg)
- **[FACT]** Excavations on the Hohenburg have run since 1981.
  - Source: Burgenwelt ; en.wikipedia
- **[FACT]** Political history of the 19th century:
  - French rule from the 1790s, in the Département Donnersberg.
  - Bavarian **Rheinkreis** from 1 May 1816.
  - J. G. A. Wirth moved his liberal newspaper to Homburg in 1831 at the invitation of Philipp Jakob Siebenpfeiffer. The two initiated the **Hambacher Fest** in 1832.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg
- **Gap:** No page I opened gave plague or famine figures for Homburg itself.
  - Regional context: the DHM essay says the "bedrückenden Auswirkungen des Dreißigjährigen Krieges" (crushing effects of the Thirty Years' War) were what ended witch persecution in the Saar region (see 4.3).

## 3. The Merburg (Kirrberg)

- **[LEGEND]** Tradition says the castle was built in the Salian period, between 1024 and 1080, on the **Malafelsen** rock. The German Wikipedia article flags this as tradition, not a documented date.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merburg
- **[FACT]** Parts date to the early 12th century, probably on the site of an older 10th-century refuge castle.
  - A Count **Dietrich von Merburg** is recorded in 1179. "Ritter von der Merburg" (ministerials, i.e. unfree knights in service) are mentioned in 1172.
  - It was probably abandoned in the late 12th century. The Pfälzisches Burgenlexikon, as reported, says the counts moved to Homburg toward the end of the 13th century.
  - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merburg ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merburg ; https://www.rheinpfalz.de/lokal/pirmasens_artikel,-verlassen-und-in-brand-gesteckt-_arid,266924.html
- **[FACT]** The Merburg lost its importance after a dispute between Emperor Barbarossa and Simon of Saarbrücken. After that the salt road no longer ran through the Lambsbach valley.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirrberg_(Homburg)
- **[FACT]** Today it is a small ruin, conserved 1975–1980. It was first excavated in the 1970s.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merburg ; Rheinpfalz (above)
- **Warning:** A web claim that the Merburg was burned and razed by Barbarossa in 1168 is false. It comes from a comment about a different castle, the Steinenschloss.
  - Checked at: https://www.komoot.com/ja-jp/guide/316509
- **No legend** about the Merburg was found in any source I opened.

## 4. Legends, folklore and witch trials

### 4.1 Legends near the project area (Saarpfalz-Kreis and Saarland)
- **[LEGEND] Giant Kreuzmann, the "Stiefel" and the Spellenstein (St. Ingbert, Saarpfalz-Kreis).**
  - "Der böse Riese Kreuzmann" lived at the Stiefel rock between Rentrisch and Ensheim. He hunted people and locked them in a cage.
  - When some escaped, he hurled his huge **Wetzstein** (whetstone) after them. It flew through the air and stuck in the ground at Rentrisch.
  - That stone is the menhir **Spellenstein**, still standing in a front garden. The city runs a children's Actionbound trail about it.
  - Sources: https://www.rheinpfalz.de/lokal/saarland_artikel,-mit-dem-smartphone-im-wald-auf-menschenfresserjagd-_arid,5237847.html ; https://www.saarpfalz-touristik.de/erlebnisse/familienzeit/aktiv-in-der-natur/st-ingberter-sagenwege-1
  - The other St. Ingbert "Sagenwege" titles are: Mori-Taten-Weg, Zauberkristall, Fröschenpfuhl, Gebrannter Wald. Their plots were not on the page.
- **[FACT] Spellenstein:**
  - Late Neolithic, about 1800 BC; 5.05 m above ground; 15–20 t.
  - Old names: "Krimhildespill" and "Grimolde Pfeil". Tilemann Stella mapped it as "Pirmansstein" in 1564.
  - It marked a boundary of the escort right on the old Geleitstraße.
  - A tradition links it to the Stiefelfelsen, whose "toe" points at the stone.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spellenstein
- **[FACT] Gollenstein (Blieskastel):**
  - Neolithic, about 2000 BC; **6.58 m**, the largest menhir in Central Europe.
  - In **1939** Wehrmacht engineers feared French artillery would use it as an aiming point and took it down. The rope snapped and it broke into four large pieces. It was re-erected with concrete in November 1951.
  - Below its niche (probably cut around 1809) are relief fragments of a figure. These are possibly a prehistoric deity resembling the thunder god Taranis.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gollenstein ; https://www.memotransfront.uni-saarland.de/pdf/westwall.pdf
  - The second source says it broke into three parts.
- **[LEGEND] The Wild Hunt in Saarland.** German Wikipedia gives a Saarland legend without a footnote: its leader is the "wilde Jäger" **Maltitz**, damned to lead the Wild Hunt forever for having hunted on Good Friday.
  - General motifs:
    - it rides during the Rauhnächte, the nights between Christmas and Epiphany;
    - its din foretells war or disease;
    - onlookers who mock it are harmed or dragged along;
    - its hounds and horses are the souls of the dead.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilde_Jagd
  - This motif fits a hunting-mad duke perfectly, but no source links Karl II. August to it. That link would be **[FICTION]**.
- **[LEGEND / tourist claim] "Stumpfer Gipfel" near the Schlossberg.**
  - The city's hiking leaflet says the small plateau "probably" served as a sacrificial site. It says hollows in a rock block resemble "einen Sitz für einen Druidenpriester" (a seat for a druid priest), ringed by huge oaks.
  - This is tourism framing, not archaeology.
  - Source: https://www.homburg.de/media/scllyew0/schlossbergtour.pdf
- **[LEGEND] Treasure-skittle legends in the Pfalz (Wasgau).**
  - Burg Drachenfels near Busenberg: a silver skittle set lies in the castle well, guarded by a fire-breathing toad. Three women in white have been seen there.
  - Burg Altdahn: ghosts play with a golden skittle set.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liste_der_Sagen_vom_Schatzkegelspiel
  - Both are outside Homburg, but the motif is regional.
- **Gap:** I found **no sourced Homburg-, Karlsberg- or Kirrberg-specific ghost story**. That includes a "Schlossgeist", a "Weiße Frau" (white lady) or a treasure tale.
  - SR states that myths surround Karlsberg but names none beyond the Versailles comparison.
  - Leads for a library visit:
    - Wilhelm Weber, *Schloss Karlsberg – Legende und Wirklichkeit* (1987);
    - Reiner Marx, *Der Karlsberg und Karl II. August in der Literatur* (2010);
    - Karl Lohmeyer, *Die Sagen der Saar*.

### 4.2 Literary atmosphere for the caves
- **[FACT]** Gustav Regler called the Schlossberg caves **"Verliese des Elends"** ("dungeons of misery") in *Im Kreuzfeuer* (1934).
  - Source: https://www.literaturland-saar.de/orte/homburg/

### 4.3 Witch trials (regional framing only)
- **[FACT]** Rita Voltmer's essay for the Deutsches Historisches Museum (DHM) "Hexenwahn" exhibition gives:
  - about **470 executions** in the Saar region plus the Lorraine bailliage d'Allemagne in the 16th–17th centuries;
  - **more than 2,000** people burned in the Duchy of Lorraine, a rough estimate;
  - main waves around 1580–1600, 1610–1615 and 1630–1650;
  - that persecution in the Saar region ended through the effects of the Thirty Years' War.
  - Victims were mostly older lower-class women. "Accusation cartels" (village committees that drove the prosecutions) existed in the Saar region.
  - Source: https://www.dhm.de/archiv/ausstellungen/hexenwahn/aufsaetze/05.htm
- **Relevance:** Homburg was under Nassau-Saarbrücken in these decades, and under Lorraine occupation from 1641 to 1679.
- **Gap:** I found no documented trial in Homburg or Kirrberg itself. The essay does not mention Pfalz-Zweibrücken. Use only as regional background.

## 5. Schlossberghöhlen (Homburg sandstone caves)

- **[FACT]** The caves are man-made. Buntsandstein, a red sandstone, does not form natural karst caves.
  - Possible early-medieval escape tunnels for the Hohenburg are uncertain.
  - The sand was mined for glassmaking, then as scouring sand and foundry moulding sand.
  - The fortress used the caves for storage.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlossberghöhlen
- **[FACT]** **1671**: first mention of a "Contre-Mine" (a counter-mine tunnel).
  - **1708**: a French engineer officer reported damaged tunnels and **spoiled ammunition and food**.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlossberghöhlen
- **[FACT]** **1872**: the authorities ordered the caves backfilled.
  - **June 1893**: first survey, by mine surveyor Spary. Sand extraction stopped and the caves were sealed again.
  - **1932**: rediscovered after being forgotten. Tourist visits from 1932, electric lighting from 1935.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlossberghöhlen
- **[FACT]** In **WWII** they were the town's air-raid shelter, including during the raid of 14 March 1945.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlossberghöhlen ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg
- **[FACT]** In the early **1950s** a secret bunker for the Saar government of Johannes Hoffmann was built in the lower **nine** levels. A hotel (now the Schlossberghotel) was built on top as cover. Work stopped when Hoffmann resigned in 1955.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlossberghöhlen
- **[FACT]** Size:
  - about **5,000 m** of passages; about 800 m open to visitors;
  - **12 levels**, about 140 × 60 m;
  - described as Europe's largest Buntsandstein caves;
  - a constant temperature of about **10 °C**.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlossberghöhlen ; https://www.homburg.de/media/scllyew0/schlossbergtour.pdf
- **[FACT]** In **2003** a large mass of sand fell from the ceiling of the "Thronsaal" (throne room) and the caves closed. They reopened as a show mine from 2007, with steel frames and new electrics.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlossberghöhlen
- **[FACT, pop culture]** In Philip Kerr's novels, detective Bernie Gunther hides in sandstone tunnels near the Karlsberg brewery.
  - Source: https://www.literaturland-saar.de/orte/homburg/

## 6. Prehistory, Celts and Romans

- **[FACT] Rabenhorst burial mounds (Homburg).**
  - In 2002 the city's department of history and monument care excavated **one of nine mounds**.
  - Mound 1:
    - 18.5 m across, 2 m high, with a 1.2 m wide ring ditch;
    - **12 graves** from the late Hallstatt to early La Tène period (Early Iron Age, roughly the 5th century BC; KuLaDig dates the site "before 500 BC");
    - 8 graves with rich bronze jewellery;
    - the dead lay in wooden coffins under sandstone slabs, and only scraps of bone survive in the sandy soil.
  - The central **grave 10** held a man with a bronze upper-arm ring (a status symbol) and two fibulae (brooches).
  - Graves 1, 2, 4, 9 and 11 were women, with paired arm and ankle rings.
  - **Grave 6** was oriented west–east and had **no grave goods**. It was covered with fragments of a **stele**: still 2.5 m tall without its tip, it is **one of the largest Celtic stelae in Central Europe**. The stele probably stood on grave 10, fell, broke, and was reused as the cover of grave 6.
  - Source: https://www.kuladig.de/Objektansicht/KLD-354061 (print version: https://www.kuladig.de/Objektansicht/Drucken/KLD-354061)
- **[FACT] Kirrberg.**
  - Stone Age hoard finds point to a trade route through the Lambsbach valley.
  - Hillfort traces dated 1000–800 BC are visible on the northern heights. Wikipedia calls them "early Celtic"; strictly, that is the Urnfield period, a Late Bronze Age culture.
  - First written mention: 1290, "Capella in Kirchperch".
  - Kirrberg joined the Saarland only on 23 April 1949, "the youngest village" of the Saarland.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirrberg_(Homburg) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirrberg_(Saar)
- **[FACT] Römermuseum Schwarzenacker.** The site is a Roman vicus (small town):
  - founded under Augustus, with more than 2,000 inhabitants and 25–30 ha;
  - only about 1 ha excavated; its ancient name is unknown;
  - **destroyed around 275 by the Alamanni**. English Wikipedia says spring 276, by Alamanni and Franks, briefly resettled, then abandoned.
  - Finds and features:
    - the "Haus des Augenarztes" (house of the eye doctor), with wall paintings and underfloor heating;
    - the tavern of Capitolinus;
    - a Jupiter giant column, exposed by a storm in 1922;
    - a bronze **pentagon dodecahedron** found near **sacrificial shafts** in 1980. Its function is still unknown.
  - The excavations were led by Alfons Kolling from 1966. The baroque Edelhaus holds Mannlich paintings.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Römermuseum_Schwarzenacker ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ein%C3%B6d
- **[FACT]** In the Middle Ages, monks of Wörschweiler Abbey ploughing their fields found the ruins and took them for Roman.
  - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ein%C3%B6d

## 7. 20th century (factual, age-appropriate framing)

- **[FACT] The West Wall (Westwall) in the Saarland.**
  - Scale: about **4,100 bunkers**, 340 minefields, 100 km of anti-tank ditches and 60 km of dragon's-teeth lines (rows of concrete anti-tank blocks). A quarter of all West Wall works stood in the Saarland section.
  - **Homburg** was the seat of Pionierstab 17 and of an Organisation Todt site office (Oberbauleitung).
  - The Saarland section ran from **Webenheim**, near Homburg, via Blieskastel and Saarbrücken to Völklingen.
  - About **8,000 plots of land** were in effect expropriated. Orchards were cut down and fields split by tank obstacles.
  - Bunkers were camouflaged as houses. Example: Am Schloß 11 in Blieskastel, on the site of a palace that was also destroyed in 1793.
  - Thousands of conscripted workers lived in some 200 barrack camps. A **police detention camp, "Homburg-Nord"**, served the Homburg site office.
  - Source: https://www.memotransfront.uni-saarland.de/pdf/westwall.pdf
- **[FACT] The "Rote Zone" (red zone).**
  - A strip about 10 km deep from the border was the red zone. A green zone about 20 km deep lay behind it.
  - Evacuation order: **3 September 1939**. An order of 2 September had been withdrawn.
  - Official terms were "Freimachung" (clearing) and "Rückführung" (repatriation).
  - About **300,000 people** left the Saarland. They could return only in summer 1940.
  - A second, chaotic evacuation followed in **November–December 1944**.
  - Before the war began, engineers blew up landmarks in the clearance zone that might help enemy artillery, such as the Hindenburgturm and churches. The Gollenstein was felled for the same reason.
  - Source: https://www.memotransfront.uni-saarland.de/pdf/westwall.pdf
  - **Gap:** I found no page I opened that says whether Homburg or Kirrberg were in the red or the green zone. Verify before using; Stadtarchiv Homburg is the best lead.
- **[FACT] 1944–45.**
  - Homburg suffered heavy damage from air raids in 1944/45.
  - The heaviest raid, by the RAF and USAAF, was on **14 March 1945**: **220 dead**. People sheltered in the Schlossberghöhlen.
  - US troops entered from Limbach on **20 March 1945**, and looting followed.
  - Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg
- **[FACT]** People in Einöd also sheltered in local caves in 1944/45.
  - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ein%C3%B6d
- **[FACT] Post-war.**
  - French zone, then the Saar Protectorate (1946–56).
  - In 1947 a French university institute opened in Homburg, the seed of the Universität des Saarlandes.
  - The secret government bunker in the caves dates from the early 1950s.
  - Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg ; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlossberghöhlen
- **Tone note for the GDD:** Present the West Wall and the evacuation through objects and everyday life (concrete blocks in the woods, a family's evacuation suitcase, a closed village). Do not gamify the war or the Nazi regime. Mention the detention camp, if at all, only as a factual memorial note, not as a gameplay space.

## 10. Story hooks (all [FICTION], each tied to the fact it grows from)

1. **"Die Meute des Hundskarl"** (The Hundskarl's pack). A spectral par-force hunt rides the Karlsberg forest in the Rauhnächte. The player must lie flat or hide, as the Wild Hunt rules say.
   - *Inspired by:* the duke's "regiments of hunting dogs", the nickname "Hundskarl" (1.2) and the Saarland Wild-Hunt legend of Maltitz (4.1).
2. **"Der Warner von Rohrbach"** (The warner from Rohrbach). A timed night-escape quest: play Nikel Pfeiffer racing to warn the duke on 9 February 1793. Choice: warn him, or let the French come?
   - *Inspired by:* the real warning and the flight via Kaiserslautern (1.5). The "lime fires" detail is tradition, not fact.
3. **"100 Wagen Stroh"** (100 wagons of straw). On 28 July 1793 the player smuggles one object out of the burning palace: a painting, a pipe head or a menagerie animal. The item turns up "today" in a Munich museum.
   - *Inspired by:* the plunder and fire (1.6) and the scattered furnishings and Alte Pinakothek (1.7).
4. **"Tränenberg"** (Hill of tears). Ghostly craftsmen still wait for their wages. Find the lost pay-ledger fragments to let them rest.
   - *Inspired by:* the duke's debts (1.2) and the "Tränenberg" nickname, which is a **[LEGEND]**.
5. **"Die Steine wandern"** (The wandering stones). A collectible chain: one marked block travels from the Hohenburg (quarried after 1714/1755) into Karlsberg (1780s), then into a Homburg house (after 1793). The player traces it through the era layers.
   - *Inspired by:* the Hohenburg used as a quarry for Karlsberg (2) and the Karlsberg ruin as a "Baustoffhandlung" (1.7).
6. **"Die Menagerie bricht aus"** (The menagerie escapes). After the fire, a jaguar, two dromedaries and a cassowary roam the Lambsbach valley. Lighter, comic-dark quests round them up.
   - *Inspired by:* the real Karlslust animal list (1.3) and the chaos of 1793 (1.6).
7. **"Das Teufelsloch"** (The devil's hole). Before the palace, the valley had an ominous name. A pre-1777 era quest finds out why.
   - *Inspired by:* the documented former name "Teufelsloch" (1.4).
8. **"Feuerwerk, 19. Dezember 1785"** (Fireworks, 19 December 1785). A sombre memorial quest: name the unnamed victims of the Karlsberg fireworks explosion. Handle it gently.
   - *Inspired by:* the fact in 1.4.
9. **"Die Stele vom Rabenhorst"** (The stele of the Rabenhorst). Why does grave 6 have no grave goods, lie west–east, and sit under the fallen stele? Celtic-era mystery; the answer stays ambiguous.
   - *Inspired by:* the 2002 excavation findings (6).
10. **"Das Zwölfeck"** (The twelve-sided object). Players find the Schwarzenacker dodecahedron near the sacrificial shafts. Its unknown purpose becomes a puzzle key across eras.
    - *Inspired by:* the 1980 find (6).
11. **"Der letzte Tag des Vicus"** (The last day of the vicus). Survive the Alamanni raid of AD 275/276 in Schwarzenacker. Hide the household gods; the player meets them again in the museum "today".
    - *Inspired by:* the destruction date (6).
12. **"Verliese des Elends"** (Dungeons of misery). Twelve levels of caves, each era sealing the last:
    - fortress stores ruined by damp (1708);
    - backfilled (1872); forgotten, then rediscovered (1932);
    - air-raid shelter (1945); a secret government bunker (1950s);
    - the Thronsaal collapse (2003).
    - *Inspired by:* section 5.
13. **"Kreuzmanns Wetzstein"** (Kreuzmann's whetstone). The giant's thrown whetstone is a menhir. A side-quest follows "stones that were thrown, felled and glued back together": Spellenstein, then the Gollenstein, broken in 1939 and re-erected in 1951.
    - *Inspired by:* the Kreuzmann **[LEGEND]** and the Gollenstein **[FACT]** (4.1).
14. **"Vauban baut, Rijswijk reißt ab"** (Vauban builds, Rijswijk tears down). Build the fortress in one era, demolish it in 1697, rebuild it, demolish it again in 1714. Uses the era-layer mechanic.
    - *Inspired by:* the Hohenburg fortress timeline (2).
15. **"Die leeren Dörfer"** (The empty villages). A quiet, non-combat 1939 layer. A child's evacuation suitcase is found in an empty village and must be "returned" in summer 1940. Concrete anti-tank blocks stand in the meadows.
    - *Inspired by:* the red-zone evacuation and the West Wall (7). Keep it age-appropriate and avoid any Nazi iconography.
